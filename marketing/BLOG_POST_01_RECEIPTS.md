# The internet has enough opinions. It needs better receipts.

Every day, millions of people tap an answer in a social poll. The result looks precise: 63% chose one option, 37% chose the other. Then the poll disappears into the feed.

What did that number actually measure?

Usually, we do not know who had a chance to participate, where respondents came from, whether they saw the result before answering, whether coordinated activity changed the outcome, or whether the wording quietly pushed people toward one choice. A percentage without this context is easy to share and hard to trust.

Pollrr starts from a simple premise: online opinion can be fast and engaging without pretending to be more scientific than it is.

## Answer first, then see the room

Pollrr hides the live result until a person answers. That makes the first decision independent of the displayed crowd. It does not eliminate every source of bias, but it removes one obvious source of social pressure from the experience.

The reveal also makes a better piece of content. A creator can ask a question, invite the audience to commit, and then discuss the result with people who have already considered their own position.

## A result should explain what it is

An open link shared on social media is not automatically a representative survey. It is a convenience sample of the people who encountered the link and chose to answer. That result may still be interesting and useful—especially to the creator who knows the audience—but it needs the correct label.

Pollrr is being built to preserve the information that usually disappears:

- the exact version of the question and choices;
- the human response count;
- when responses were collected;
- which distribution sources contributed responses;
- what methodology and integrity rules were active;
- and a signed aggregate record that can be checked later.

The public can inspect enough to understand the result without exposing respondent identities or publishing the row-level data that makes a research network valuable.

## Numbers are only half the opinion

A binary result shows direction. It rarely shows reasoning.

Pollrr lets respondents optionally explain what mattered most. Those explanations can reveal that people who chose opposite answers still share a concern about cost, fairness, safety, local control, or institutional trust. The goal is not to force agreement. It is to show the structure underneath disagreement.

AI can help organize those explanations, but every summary must remain connected to counts and the verified human sample. “Eight percent of explanations mentioned cost” is not the same claim as “eight percent of all voters chose based on cost.” Pollrr is designed to preserve that distinction.

## AI should interpret, not impersonate

AI is useful for spotting loaded wording, suggesting clearer answer choices, finding themes in explanations, and turning a verified result into a concise creator-ready summary.

AI is not a respondent.

Pollrr keeps measured human votes separate from modeled estimates. An AI-generated estimate may someday be useful as a clearly labeled analytical layer, but it should never be silently added to a human total or presented as people who participated.

That line is both a product decision and a trust promise: AI may help us understand the room; it does not get a seat in the headcount.

## Built for people with audiences

Creators and publishers already ask their communities questions. Pollrr gives that behavior a stronger home: free poll creation, compact distribution links, channel attribution, post-vote results, explanations, and a record worth citing later.

One good question can become several useful pieces of work: the original prompt, the result reveal, an explanation of why each side answered as it did, a follow-up question, and a longer-running view of how the audience changes over time.

The poll is free because distribution matters. The long-term product is a trustworthy, longitudinal understanding of human opinion—not a paywall around a two-button form.
